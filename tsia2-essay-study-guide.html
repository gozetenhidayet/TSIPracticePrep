TSIA2 Essay: From a Position to a Developed Argument

Make a claim you can explain

An argument does more than announce a preference. It gives reasons, develops examples, and explains how those examples support a position. A useful thesis names both the decision and its limits. “Libraries are good” is too broad to guide an essay. “Our town should extend library hours twice a week, provided a trial shows enough use to justify the cost” gives the writer a clear argument to develop.

Original practice prompt

A town has limited funds for its public library. Some residents want later weekday opening hours; others prefer spending the money on additional books and digital resources. Which investment should the town prioritize? Develop a position with reasons and specific examples, and address a reasonable objection.

Plan before drafting

  • Position: test later hours on two evenings before committing the full budget.
  • Reason one: access depends on when people are free, not just on what the library owns.
  • Reason two: a limited trial produces information for a better spending decision.
  • Objection: staffing and security may consume money that could serve more readers through digital resources.
  • Response: measure attendance and costs, then adjust or end the trial if benefits are too small.

Model argument

A public library’s collection matters only when residents can use it. For a town deciding between later weekday hours and additional resources, the best first investment is a limited trial of evening access. Opening two evenings a week would help residents whose work or family responsibilities conflict with the current schedule. A trial would also let the town test that benefit before making an expensive permanent commitment.

First, later hours can make existing resources useful to people who currently miss them. Consider a resident who works until five thirty and reaches a library that closes at six. Even if the library purchases more books, that resident still has little time to browse, use a computer, or ask for research help. Two later evenings could provide a predictable window for those activities. This example illustrates a problem of access rather than a shortage of materials: increasing a collection does not automatically remove a scheduling barrier.

Second, a trial can replace assumptions with local evidence. The library could count evening visits, record study-room use, and compare added staffing costs with the services people actually use. It could also ask nonusers whether the new hours solve a practical problem for them. These steps would not guarantee a successful program, but they would make the next budget decision more informed. If only a few residents attend, the town could redirect the remaining money.

Supporters of digital resources raise a serious objection. Online materials can be available after closing without requiring a staffed building, and they may reach many residents at a lower cost. That advantage should shape the trial, not be dismissed. The library could begin with only two evenings, keep a clear spending limit, and compare the results with demand for digital materials. Residents who need a quiet room or reliable internet access may benefit from the building even when online books are available.

The town should therefore prioritize a modest trial of later hours rather than an immediate expansion across every weekday. That approach recognizes both the value of access and the limits of the budget. If attendance and feedback show a meaningful benefit, the town can continue the hours. If they do not, it can invest more confidently in the collection and digital services.

Why the argument develops

The introduction takes a position with a condition. The second paragraph uses a hypothetical resident rather than inventing a study or statistic. It then explains why that example matters. The third paragraph proposes observable evidence without pretending the evidence has already been collected. The counterargument acknowledges a real benefit of digital services and responds with a limited proposal. The conclusion follows from those reasons instead of adding a new unsupported claim.

Revise a weak paragraph

Before: “Later hours are better. Everyone needs the library. Books are important, and it is obvious that the town should do this. People will like it.”

The paragraph repeats its opinion but supplies no specific situation or reasoning. “Everyone” and “obvious” claim more certainty than the writer has established.

See one stronger revision

“Later hours could help residents whose work schedules overlap with the library’s opening times. For example, a parent who finishes work at six may need an evening place to help a child research a school project. Extending access twice a week would make the existing books, computers, and staff assistance available to that family. The benefit would depend on actual attendance, so the town should evaluate a trial before making the schedule permanent.”

The revision adds a situation, connects it to the policy, and qualifies the conclusion. It supports the original idea without inventing facts.

Use this checklist on your own draft

  1. Underline your thesis. Does it answer the prompt rather than merely restate the topic?
  2. Label each body paragraph’s main reason. Combine paragraphs that repeat the same point.
  3. Circle each example. Add a sentence explaining how it supports your reason.
  4. Identify a reasonable objection and answer it fairly.
  5. Check sentence boundaries: two complete sentences need appropriate punctuation or a conjunction.
  6. Check agreement: “Each proposal has benefits,” not “Each proposal have benefits.”
  7. Remove invented statistics and claims that you cannot support. A clearly hypothetical example can still develop an argument.
  8. Read the conclusion beside the thesis. They should express a consistent position.

For another session, argue the opposite position on the same prompt. Develop the strongest case for digital resources instead of simply changing a few words. This exercise helps you practice flexible reasoning rather than memorizing one essay.
