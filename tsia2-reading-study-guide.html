TSIA2 Reading: Main Idea, Evidence, and Inference

Read an original passage, answer five questions, and compare every answer choice with the evidence. No account is required. Work on paper, then open the solutions to check your reasoning.

MethodExamplesSelf-checkNext steps

Read for relationships, not isolated words

A main idea combines the topic with what the author says about it. A detail is one supporting fact. An inference is a conclusion supported by the text even when it is not stated word for word. Keep these tasks separate: a true detail can still be the wrong answer to a main-idea question.

Read the passage once for its overall direction. Note changes signaled by words such as “however,” “because,” and “although.” Then answer each question using a specific sentence or relationship. Outside knowledge may be interesting, but it cannot substitute for evidence in the passage.

Original practice passage: a later library opening

For years, the Cedar Street Library closed at six each weekday. A student advisory group proposed keeping it open until eight on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Members argued that students with afternoon jobs often arrived too late to use the quiet study rooms. Rather than change the permanent schedule immediately, the library ran a six-week trial.

During the trial, visits between six and eight averaged twenty-four per evening. Study rooms were usually occupied, but borrowing at the front desk changed little. Staff also recorded additional security and utility costs. A survey at the entrance received enthusiastic responses, although the director noted that it mostly reached people already using the new hours.

The director recommended continuing the later hours for another term while contacting residents who had not visited. She explained that the trial showed a demand for evening study space, but it did not yet establish whether those benefits justified a permanent change. The advisory group agreed to help collect broader feedback.

Answer before opening each explanation

1. Which statement best expresses the main idea?

  1. Libraries should stop lending books and offer only study rooms.
  2. A trial of later library hours showed benefits but left questions for further evaluation.
  3. The survey proved all residents supported later hours.
  4. Additional security costs forced the library to end evening access.
Answer and reasoning

B. This covers both the demand for study space and the remaining uncertainty. A invents a change the passage never proposes. C ignores the director’s concern about whom the survey reached. D contradicts the recommendation to continue the trial. Notice that the correct answer includes the passage’s qualification rather than claiming the proposal completely succeeded or failed.

2. Why did the director question the survey’s reach?

  1. It mainly collected responses from people already attending during the new hours.
  2. It contained no questions about library services.
  3. Staff refused to record the answers.
  4. The student group opposed public feedback.
Answer and reasoning

A. The passage explicitly says the survey mostly reached current users of the new hours. Their views may differ from those of nonusers. B and C have no textual support. D conflicts with the group’s agreement to help collect broader feedback. A sample can supply useful information without representing everyone.

3. What can reasonably be inferred about the later visits?

  1. Every visitor was a student with a job.
  2. Book borrowing doubled during the trial.
  3. Study-space access was an important reason for evening attendance.
  4. No visitor used the library before six.
Answer and reasoning

C. Usually occupied study rooms, combined with little change in borrowing, support this inference. A and D use absolute statements that the passage cannot establish. B contradicts the borrowing observation. A good inference connects the evidence without adding an unsupported number, motive, or universal claim.

4. In the final paragraph, “establish” most nearly means:

  1. Build a physical structure.
  2. Demonstrate with sufficient evidence.
  3. Appoint a staff member.
  4. Write down opening hours.
Answer and reasoning

B. Substitute “demonstrate with sufficient evidence” into the sentence: the trial did not yet demonstrate whether benefits justified a permanent change. A is a possible meaning in other contexts, but no building is being constructed here. C and D do not fit the sentence’s argument.

5. What role does the final paragraph play?

  1. It introduces an unrelated dispute.
  2. It dismisses the trial’s findings.
  3. It announces that evening hours are permanent.
  4. It proposes a next step that responds to both the findings and their limits.
Answer and reasoning

D. Continuing the hours responds to the observed demand; contacting nonusers addresses the survey’s limitation. A is wrong because the same proposal remains the focus. B overlooks the director’s acknowledgment of demand. C confuses a further term of evaluation with a permanent decision.

A practical review routine

For each missed question, write three short notes: what the question asked, the sentence that supports the answer, and why your choice went beyond or fell short of that evidence. If you chose an answer with “all,” “never,” or “proved,” check whether the passage was equally certain. Strong wording is not automatically wrong, but it requires strong support.

For your next passage, summarize each paragraph in five to ten words before looking at the questions. Then combine those notes into one balanced sentence. This helps distinguish a central claim from an attractive detail. Do not turn the exercise into a speed test until you can justify your answers reliably.
