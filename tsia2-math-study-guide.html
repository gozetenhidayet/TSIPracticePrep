TSIA2 Math: Build a Solution, Then Check It

A reliable method for unfamiliar problems

A correct calculation is useful only if it answers the question asked. Before choosing an operation, identify the unknown, write down its unit, and decide which information connects to it. For a price problem, distinguish the original price, the amount of a discount, and the final amount paid. For a geometry problem, distinguish a length from an area. That short setup prevents many avoidable mistakes.

  1. Represent: define a variable or draw a labeled diagram.
  2. Relate: write the equation, ratio, or probability before substituting numbers.
  3. Solve: keep units and signs visible through each step.
  4. Check: substitute your result into the original relationship and estimate whether its size makes sense.

Five worked examples

1. Ratios: distinguish a part from the whole

A school orders blue and green folders in a ratio of 3:5. It orders 96 folders altogether. How many are green?

The ratio contains 3 + 5 = 8 equal parts. Each part represents 96 ÷ 8 = 12 folders. The green share is 5 × 12 = 60 folders. Check: 36 blue + 60 green = 96, and 36:60 reduces to 3:5.

Common mistake: dividing 96 by 5 treats all 96 folders as the green share. A total must be divided by the total number of ratio parts. A helpful diagram is eight equal boxes, with five labeled green.

2. Percent changes: the base matters

A $80 backpack is discounted by 25%. An 8% sales tax is then applied to the discounted price. What is the final price?

A 25% discount leaves 75% of the original amount: 80 × 0.75 = 60. Tax applies to $60, so the final price is 60 × 1.08 = $64.80. The tax itself is $4.80. This result is below $80, as expected after a substantial discount.

Common mistake: subtracting 25% and adding 8% to get a net 17% reduction. Those percentages refer to different bases. Apply consecutive changes as separate multipliers.

3. Equations: translate a fixed fee and a variable charge

A bicycle rental costs $12 plus $7 per hour. A customer pays $47. For how many hours did the customer rent the bicycle?

Let h be the number of hours. The fixed charge is paid once, so 12 + 7h = 47. Subtract 12: 7h = 35. Divide by 7: h = 5 hours. Check: 12 + 7(5) = 47.

Common mistake: writing 12h + 7 or dividing the whole bill by 7. Identify which amount changes with time before forming the equation.

4. Geometry: find dimensions before area

A rectangle has perimeter 42 meters. Its length is 3 meters greater than its width. Find its area.

Let the width be w and the length be w + 3. Then 2w + 2(w + 3) = 42. Distribute and combine: 4w + 6 = 42, so w = 9 and the length is 12. The area is 9 × 12 = 108 square meters. Check the perimeter: 2(9) + 2(12) = 42 meters.

Common mistake: reporting 42 or 21 as the area. Perimeter measures the boundary in meters; area measures the interior in square meters.

5. Probability: update the sample space

A bag contains 4 red and 6 blue tokens. Two tokens are drawn without replacement. What is the probability that both are red?

The first red probability is 4/10. After a red token is removed, 3 red tokens remain among 9 tokens. Multiply: (4/10)(3/9) = 12/90 = 2/15. The second draw depends on the first because the token is not replaced.

Common mistake: using 4/10 twice. That would describe drawing with replacement, a different experiment.

Try these before revealing the solutions

Write your setup and check, not just your final answer. These are independent practice items, not official exam questions.

  1. A drink mixes concentrate and water in a 2:7 ratio. How much water is needed for 27 cups of drink?
  2. A $120 jacket is discounted 15%, then taxed 5% on its sale price. Find the total.
  3. A service costs $18 plus $4 per visit. A customer pays $54. How many visits were made?
  4. A rectangle has perimeter 34 cm and length 5 cm greater than its width. Find its area.
  5. A box has 3 white and 5 black counters. Two are drawn without replacement. Find the probability both are black.
  6. The values are 4, 6, 6, 9, 15. Find the mean and median. Which is affected more if 15 becomes 30?
Reveal the six solutions
  1. There are 9 ratio parts. Each is 27/9 = 3 cups. Water is 7 × 3 = 21 cups.
  2. 120 × 0.85 = 102; 102 × 1.05 = $107.10.
  3. 18 + 4v = 54, so 4v = 36 and v = 9.
  4. 2w + 2(w + 5) = 34 gives w = 6, length 11, and area 66 cm².
  5. (5/8)(4/7) = 20/56 = 5/14.
  6. The sum is 40, so the mean is 8; the middle value is 6. Replacing 15 with 30 changes the mean to 11; the median stays 6. The mean changes more.

Turn mistakes into your next session

If you missed ratio or percent items, practice identifying the whole and the base before calculating. If you missed equations or geometry, write the relationship in words first. If you missed probability, list what remains after the first event. Rework a missed problem without looking at the solution the next day, then solve a new problem of the same type. Correctly explaining a new problem is stronger evidence of learning than remembering an old answer.
